Natural springs are a different proposition from venues that heat and mineralise their water artificially. The source does the work: geothermal energy or mineral-rich aquifer water rises to the surface, and the venue builds around it. What you get in the water is genuinely the product of geology instead of a recipe.

That distinction matters more than it might sound. The Great Artesian Basin, for instance, feeds bore baths scattered across outback Queensland and New South Wales that are about as far from a polished day spa as you can get. Places like the Artesian Mud Baths in Eulo or the Pilliga Artesian Bore Bath are working-class infrastructure in the oldest sense: communities built around free or very cheap access to water that soaks out of the ground hot. No packaging, no ambient music. The Pilliga bath runs around the clock, every day, with a campsite attached instead of a day-pass counter. Bring your own towel.

Victoria's springs country, centred on the Mornington Peninsula and the Daylesford-Hepburn region, is a different world. Peninsula Hot Springs and Alba Thermal Springs have both scaled into large multi-pool complexes; Mineral Springs Hotel in Hepburn Springs adds a sauna and cold plunge to its spring-fed indoor pool, which sets it apart from the purely thermal venues in this table. Deep Blue in Warrnambool runs structured sessions instead of open entry, so it's worth checking timing before you turn up.

Woodlands Rainforest Retreat in Narbethong packages its springs access with massage and dining instead of selling entry alone; its pricing reflects that. The swimwear-optional dress code is also worth knowing ahead of time.

A few venues haven't published drop-in prices here, so the table will show that plainly instead of us guessing. Hours vary considerably across the set, and a handful of venues operate without bookings, which is rarer than it used to be. Check the individual listings before you drive two hours into the bush.
